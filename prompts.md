# 1. Finding land for its acquisition pipeline

Homes England buys, assembles and prepares land, much of it brownfield, before selling it to developers. Before anyone approaches a landowner, a first screen of where to look saves a lot of analyst time.

"Which MSOAs in outer London have the most brownfield land outside flood zones and protected habitats, within easy reach of a station? I want a shortlist of 20 areas to start landowner conversations."

How the engine answers it: brownfield land and station count weighted positively, with exclusions for flood zones, habitats and monuments set at around 25–50% coverage. The ranking gives the shortlist.
What's missing: land ownership, especially public-sector land from the Land Hub. Without it, the engine can't tell a council-owned car park from a site in private ownership.

# 2. Green Belt and "grey belt" screening

Since the NPPF update in December 2024, local plans can release "grey belt" land: Green Belt land that contributes little to the Green Belt's purposes. Homes England advises local authorities and partners on where growth could go.

"Show me Green Belt areas that are close to existing stations and local services, aren't in flood zones and aren't protected habitats. Which parts of the Green Belt look like the strongest candidates for a grey belt conversation with the borough?"

How the engine answers it: Green Belt coverage weighted positively, plus stations and health and education amenities, with exclusions for flood zones and habitats. This is the brief's "strategic context" layer turned into a question.
Its limit: this is a first sieve only. A real grey belt assessment checks each parcel against the Green Belt purposes, which the engine doesn't model.

# 3. Deciding where to invest in infrastructure or partnerships

Homes England puts infrastructure funding and place-based partnerships where they unlock the most homes. Analysts need to compare areas on both housing capacity and need.

"Which boroughs have a lot of brownfield capacity but weak local services, so that infrastructure investment would unlock the most homes? Then group all boroughs into 4 types so we can see which ones need a similar approach."

How the engine answers it: the Boroughs level, with brownfield land weighted positively and amenities weighted negatively to find service gaps. A follow-up in cluster mode then groups the boroughs into types. This plays to the clustering and the cluster profiles panel.
What's missing: housing need and affordability data, such as house price to earnings ratios and housing waiting lists. Without those, "where to invest" is judged on supply only.

# 4. Checking the location of a loan application

Homes England lends to developers, including SME builders. A credit analyst wants a quick, consistent check of a scheme's location against comparable areas.

"A developer has applied for a loan to build 120 homes in Bexley 012. How does that area compare with the rest of outer London on schools, health care and transport? Is any of it in a flood zone or on protected land?"

How the engine answers it: partly, today. The tooltip shows one area's values, but the agent can only see the top and bottom of a ranking, not a named zone.
What's missing: a small zone_profile tool that returns one zone's features, percentiles and constraint coverage. That would let the agent answer this properly, and it's the cheapest gap to close of the four.
